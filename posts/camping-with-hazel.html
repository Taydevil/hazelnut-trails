Camping With Hazel: A Field Guide

A summer of trips has turned our trial-and-error into an actual system. Here's what moving a two-year-old cattle dog mix through the mountains has taught us.

Know who you're dealing with

Hazel is an Australian cattle dog with a little bit of husky, and the blend shows: tireless, loyal to a fault, and loud when the moment calls for it. Meeting new people, she's nervous at first — she needs to warm up before she'll let you near her. Once she knows you, she is deeply, completely sweet. Camp is where those two instincts tug: she wants to guard everything we own, and it can take real effort to get her to settle once the sun goes down.

Bedtime rules

  • Car camping: Hazel sleeps in the car. It's her den, she's happy, and it saves everyone the midnight rustle patrol.
  • Backpacking: she's in the tent with Rachie, curled up under the blankets — a compact, lightly snoring, exceptionally warm little oven. Cold nights belong to whoever shares her corner.

The leash policy, and it's the whole policy

Hazel is always on leash when we're out. Car camping gets a long leash at camp for a bit of freedom, but the rule stands: leash on, every time. When we meet other hikers we step off the trail to make room, and sometimes we actively walk away — especially around people on horses or with off-leash dogs. It's not that we're mean. Off-leash dogs coming into our space bothers us even when the dog is perfectly friendly; it feels disrespectful of the trail, and of other campers trying to manage a nervous dog of their own.

Gear we actually pack for her

  • Her own backpack. She carries her rain jacket and a sweater in it. Yes, really — a cattle dog with a luggage allowance.
  • One collapsible water bowl. We feed and water her from the same bowl on backpacking trips so it earns its weight twice over.
  • A long leash for camp. Freedom without vanishing.
  • Patience. Not a product you can buy, but the item we reach for most.

When she's being naughty

Let's be honest about the naughty mode: won't listen, lunges at squirrels and other dogs, barks at people. That prolonged coyote-husky call of hers carries a long way and announces exactly what she thinks of the situation. We manage it with space, leash management, and walks — a tired Hazel is a polite Hazel. She's young; we're working on it, mostly by putting her in positions where the bad choice never happens.

Where the summer took us

Separate backpacking trips to Cubrant Lake, Kermsuh Lake, Hell Hole Lake, and Red Castle Lake — she chose to sleep in a tent next to Rachie at every single one. Somewhere along the way we also climbed two different peaks named "Baldy." Two Baldy summits, one dog, zero complaints about the altitude.

A tired dog is a good dog — but a well-managed one gets to come back next time.
